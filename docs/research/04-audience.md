# Designing for this specific player

One rising 6th-grade girl who has had the lessons and they didn't stick, is
self-conscious about being behind, and likes Minecraft. Her brother also plays.

Generalisations about "girls' games" are mostly worthless. These findings are
included because they're specific and they change design decisions.

---

## Math anxiety peaks in 6th grade, and it's higher in girls

This is not a soft finding and it lands exactly on her:

- Math anxiety is higher in female students than male students and **peaks in
  sixth grade** (Frontiers in Psychology).
- The mechanism is working-memory occupancy: fear of confirming a stereotype
  "evokes negative thoughts and ruminations, leaving less working memory
  available for the math task at hand." The anxiety literally consumes the
  capacity the maths needs.
- In experiments where stereotype threat was removed, girls' underperformance
  **disappeared entirely**. It is a state, not a trait.

**Design consequences, in order of importance:**

1. **Never display a grade level, age norm, or comparison to "should."** The
   build notes already got this right for the tutor. It matters more in a game,
   where a "Grade 4" label on a level select is devastating.
2. **Never put a clock on a single question.** Timed drilling is the best-
   documented way to manufacture math anxiety, especially early in learning.
3. **Pressure from the world, not from the question.** Number Munchers had
   monsters walking toward you — urgency without a stopwatch on your thinking.
   That distinction is the whole game-design trick here.
4. **Failure must be cheap and instant.** Respawn, retry, no score penalty, no
   "incorrect" sound. She should be able to be wrong twenty times in a minute
   without it meaning anything.

The corollary for you: parental math anxiety transmits. Girls paired with a
high-math-anxiety teacher performed worse; the same effect shows up
parent-to-child. Playing these *with* her cheerfully is part of the intervention.

## Timed practice isn't banned — it's re-framed

The fluency research is not "never time anything." It's that timing must be
**low-stakes and self-referential**: the clock exists so *she* can watch her own
number improve, never to rank her or gate her.

Games get this almost for free — the same time pressure "feels less like being on
the clock" inside a game. That's the honest argument for the arcade formats.

Also worth taking from the fluency literature: **interleaving** beats blocking.
Rotating between skills in variable order outperforms mastering one then moving
on. The tutor's 21 topics are currently blocked by topic; a game session that
mixes is doing something the tutor can't.

## What "likes Minecraft" actually tells us

Minecraft is a *building* game with a resource economy. The pull is authorship
and accumulation, not combat. Reinforced by the general finding that girls' game
preferences skew toward creativity, storytelling, and process over competition
and win-states.

So: **build-and-own beats beat-the-level.** A game where the reward is a
structure that persists and is hers will outperform a game where the reward is a
score. This also happens to be the single best math framing available — building
requires area, perimeter, volume, ratio and scale as a *side effect*, which is
exactly how Minecraft Education gets its results.

## Textless is a feature, not a constraint

Slice Fractions, DragonBox and Refraction are essentially wordless. For a kid
sensitive about being behind, no text means no reading-level signal, no
instructions that assume prior knowledge, and nothing that can be screenshotted
as babyish. It also makes the games playable by a younger sibling without
modification.

## The sibling

Two kids on the allowlist. Head-to-head maths is risky if one is visibly ahead —
it re-creates the comparison the whole design is trying to remove. Safer shapes:
co-op building on a shared world, asynchronous personal-best racing, or
**her building levels/puzzles for him** — which the CriaMat result suggests is
among the strongest learning activities available, and flips her into the
expert role.
